KNOWN CAUSES & TRIGGERS OF PGAD

PGAD/Genito-Pelvic Dysesthesia (GPD) is a physical medical condition driven by misfiring sensory pathways, structural compression, or hemodynamic congestion rather than psychological desire. Common underlying causes include:

  • Neurological & Spinal Factors: Tarlov (perineural) cysts in the sacral spine (S2–S4), lumbar/sacral disc herniations, or spinal stenosis compressing the nerve roots that supply the pelvic floor.
  • Peripheral Nerve Entrapment: Compression, irritation, or scarring of the pudendal nerve or dorsal nerve of the clitoris caused by pelvic trauma, childbirth, surgery, chronic cycling, or FGM scar neuromas.
  • Vascular Congestion: Pelvic Congestion Syndrome (PCS), pelvic varicose veins, or arteriovenous malformations that trap blood in pelvic and genital tissues.
  • Pharmacological Triggers: Initiation or sudden withdrawal of SSRIs/SNRIs, trazodone, or dopamine-modulating medications that alter spinal cord inhibitory signaling.
  • Pelvic Floor Hypertonicity: Overactive, spasming pelvic floor muscles putting mechanical pressure on adjacent nerves and blood vessels.
VASCULAR SYSTEM & PELVIC VARICOSE VEINS

Healthy genital arousal relies on a rapid cycle of increased arterial inflow followed by efficient venous drainage once stimulation ends. For individuals with varicose veins in the pelvis or vulvar region (Pelvic Congestion Syndrome), this drainage mechanism malfunctions:

  • Venous Valve Insufficiency: Normally, tiny one-way valves inside the ovarian and internal iliac veins prevent blood from flowing backward due to gravity. When these valves weaken or fail, blood flows in reverse (venous reflux).
  • Chronic Pooling & Engorgement: Reflux causes the deep pelvic veins surrounding the uterus, ovaries, vaginal walls, and clitoral bulbs to stretch, twist, and balloon into varicosities.
  • Persistent Pressure on Arousal Tissues: Because the vestibular bulbs and clitoral crura are densely vascular erectile structures, stagnant venous blood keeps them chronically engorged and hypersensitive—mimicking continuous physical arousal, throbbing, or heaviness even without sexual triggers.
  • Positional Symptoms: Vascular PGAD symptoms frequently worsen after prolonged standing, sitting, or before menstruation (when hormonal shifts further dilate blood vessels) and may temporarily ease when lying flat with legs elevated.
RECOMMENDED MEDICAL TESTING TO RULE OUT CAUSES

Because PGAD overlaps with spinal, vascular, and muscular conditions, specialists typically use a targeted diagnostic workup to identify or rule out treatable root causes:

Diagnostic Test Target System What It Rules Out / Identifies
Pelvic & Lumbosacral MRI Sacral Spine & Soft Tissue Rules out Tarlov cysts, sacral nerve root compression, herniated discs, or pelvic masses pressing on S2–S4 nerves.
MR Neurography (MRN) Peripheral Pelvic Nerves High-resolution nerve imaging used to detect pudendal nerve entrapment, thickening, or post-surgical/FGM scar neuromas.
Transvaginal & Pelvic Doppler Ultrasound Pelvic & Vulvar Vasculature Performed upright and lying down (often with Valsalva maneuver) to detect dilated pelvic varicose veins and retrograde venous blood flow.
Pelvic Venography / CT or MR Venogram Ovarian & Internal Iliac Veins Maps exact venous reflux pathways and confirms Pelvic Congestion Syndrome or vascular compressions (such as Nutcracker or May-Thurner syndrome).
Diagnostic Pudendal Nerve Block Pudendal Nerve Pathway Image-guided local anesthetic injection; temporary symptom relief confirms the pudendal nerve is the primary pain/arousal signal generator.
Quantitative Sensory Testing (QST) & Pelvic Floor Exam Sensory Fibers & Musculature Assesses small-fiber neuropathy (temperature/vibration thresholds) and evaluates myofascial trigger points or hypertonic pelvic floor muscles.
